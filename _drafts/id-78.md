_Some extracts from Pale Fire. Be warned: many spoilers._

_Pale Fire_ is a 1962 novel written by Russian-American writer Vladimir Nabokov, comprising of the titular 999-line poem by fictional poet John Shade, accompanied with a foreword and commentary by (also fictional) Charles Kinbote. While quite unseeming at first, on deeper reading it becomes plainly apparent that nothing is as it seems, and that Kinbote is obscuring details about himself and his relationship with Shade. I won't say more on that, but I do want to say a bit about the poem.

The poem itself is written in iambic pentameter with rhyming couplets and is divided into four cantos, very reminiscent of "The Rape of The Lock" by Enlightenment era poet Alexander Pope; unlike the latter, however, "Pale Fire" is roughly autobiographical, with each canto referring to a stage of Shade's life.

The thread woven throughout the entire piece is Shade's self-posed question of the nature of "survival after death", one that becomes his raison d'être during his youth, when he "decided to explore and fight / the foul, the admissible abyss / devoting all my twisted life" to finding some shred of evidence to the existence of an afterlife. In later adulthood he is further spurred on by the tragic loss of his daughter Hazel, whose wretched life and eventual suicide are the subject of the latter half of the second canto. A near-death experience he relates in Canto Three affirms his belief in the hereafter—one where he might be reunited with his daughter.

Despite line after line being crammed with allusions to other literary works I have never read and probably will never, I find the whole thing to be incredibly tender: at its most heartbreaking, Nabokov lets the simmering grief of a bereaved father still coming to terms with it seep through.

## Death and the Afterlife

Best expressed in the first quatrain, and the most memorable of the poem:

> ```
> I was the shadow of the waxwing slainBy the false azure in the windowpane;I was the smudge of ashen fluff – and ILived on, flew on, in the reflected sky.
> ```

What Kinbote describes so tersely as Shade's first experience of an "eschatological shock" not only touches on the suddenness of death—the imperceptible "windowpane"—but also reveals the post-mortal theme in terms of reflections. The poet elaborates on the "reflected sky" later on, in what's my favourite section in the poem:

> ```
> And I'll turn down eternity unlessThe melancholy and the tendernessOf mortal life; the passion and the pain;The claret taillight of that dwindling planeOff Hesperus; your gesture of dismayOn running out of cigarettes; the wayYou smile at dogs; the trail of silver slimeSnails leave on flagstones; this good ink, this rhyme,This index card, this slender rubber bandWhich always forms, when dropped, an ampersand,Are found in Heaven by the newlydeadStored in its strongholds through the years.
> ```
